Hyper-Epoch Training for Data-Constrained Scientific Pretraining

Pretraining on molecules, genomes and crystal structures is limited by the amount of unique data rather than by compute. We train with one recipe for many passes over a fixed corpus. On SMILES, it reaches the pretraining loss of the best released molecular language models with 13–39× less unique data, and the gain carries over to property prediction.

October 2026

TL;DR

  • In scientific domains, unique data is more limited than compute. New records come from synthesis, sequencing or simulation, and many repeat known structure: in a 98M-molecule SMILES corpus, the share of molecules with a previously unseen ring system falls from 4.5% to 0.2% as the corpus grows.
  • In chemistry, one hyper-epoch recipe (snapshot ensembling, chain distillation and looped depth) reaches the pretraining loss of the best released SMILES models, those of Xu et al., with 13–39× less unique data at budgets from 5M to 200M unique tokens.
  • Against our own tuned single-pass frontier, the recipe is worth 5.4–6.2× as much unique data at every budget, and tuned plain repetition is worth 2.1–3.0×. The rest of the 13–39× comes from our single-pass baseline being stronger than that of Xu et al.
  • The recipe's multiplier is flat across budgets, while that of plain repetition falls from 3.0× to 2.1× between 5M and 200M unique tokens. Extra seeds change the recipe's multiplier by less than 0.25×.
  • On a pooled score over eight MoleculeNet tasks with scaffold splits, the recipe pretrained on 50M unique tokens scores above our single-pass 3B-token model, and at 100M above the best released 3B-token model of Xu et al.
  • Ensembling accounts for most of the gain. Looping contributes more at larger budgets and distillation at smaller ones.

1Introduction

In scientific modeling, available compute grows faster than the supply of new observations. Each new molecule must be synthesised and characterised, each genome sequenced, and each crystal structure computed with density functional theory. More records also do not always add information: many new SMILES strings recombine parts already present in the corpus (Section 2). We ask how much more a model can learn from a fixed corpus by training on it for many passes.

For web text, Muennighoff et al. found that a few epochs of repeated data are nearly as useful as new data, and that further repeats are worth rapidly less. Our work on protein language models (Baron et al.) showed that a tuned multi-epoch recipe extracts considerably more from repeated data. q0 introduced hyper-epoch training, a set of primitives designed for many passes over the same data.

This post applies one hyper-epoch recipe, with the same rules, to several data-constrained domains and evaluates each in the same way. Since the recipe does not change between domains, the post is organised by question, and each table and figure has one row or panel per domain. This version reports results for molecular language models trained on SMILES, a string representation of molecular structure; results for eukaryotic DNA and crystal-structure generation will be added as further rows. In chemistry we vary the number of unique tokens from 5M to 200M and compare each repeated-data run with a tuned single-pass frontier measured up to 1.5B tokens. Each result is stated as the amount of unique data a single pass would need to match it.

Our contributions in chemistry:

  • A measurement of how quickly new structure appears in a 98M-molecule corpus: the rate of new scaffolds and ring systems falls steadily as the corpus grows.
  • Tuned baselines: a single-epoch frontier tuned over model size, learning rate and batch size (1,377 runs), and a tuned multi-epoch arm (3,648 runs).
  • One hyper-epoch recipe with preset rules across budgets that reaches the pretraining loss of the best released checkpoints of Xu et al. with 13–39× less unique data, and is worth 5.4–6.2× unique data against our own single-pass frontier, from 5M to 200M unique tokens.
  • Downstream evidence on eight MoleculeNet tasks with scaffold splits: from 50M unique tokens the recipe fine-tunes better than a single pass over 3B.
  • A leave-one-out ablation of every component, on both pretraining and fine-tuning.

2Why Pretraining Is Data-Constrained

Training compute can be increased by buying hardware. Scientific data increases only as fast as experiments and simulations produce it, and much of the new data overlaps with existing data. A scientific corpus is therefore limited in two ways: by the number of records and by the amount of new structure each additional record contains. The second limit can apply even when the corpus is large.

2.1Molecules

PubChem lists about 119 million compounds (Kim, S. et al.). Make-on-demand catalogues are larger: Enamine REAL contains more than 80 billion virtual products (Enamine). These products are enumerated from a fixed set of building blocks and about 170 validated reactions, so the size of the catalogue reflects the number of combinations rather than the amount of new chemistry.

We measured how fast new structure appears in the corpus we train on. Streaming its 97.7M deduplicated molecules in training order, we count distinct Bemis–Murcko scaffolds (Bemis and Murcko), their generic forms (atom and bond types removed) and ring systems, together with the share of molecules that contain a ring system not seen earlier (Table 1). If every molecule brought new structure, the counts would grow in proportion to the corpus. Instead they grow as $N^{0.83}$ for scaffolds, $N^{0.63}$ for generic scaffolds and $N^{0.59}$ for ring systems, and the novel-ring-system rate falls from 4.5% at 100k molecules to 0.2% at 97.7M. Of the molecules added between 30M and 97.7M, 99.8% contain only ring systems that already occur earlier in the corpus. Our budgets of 5M to 200M unique tokens (about 0.1M to 4.5M molecules) lie in the range where the novel-ring-system rate has already fallen from 4.5% to below 1%.

Labelled data is smaller still. The eight property-prediction tasks we fine-tune on (Section 7) have between 642 molecules (FreeSolv) and 7,831 (Tox21), because each label requires a measurement. Performance on these tasks therefore depends heavily on what the model learns in pretraining.

2.2Genomes and Crystal Structures

The same two limits apply to the other domains. Genomic diversity is limited by the species that have been sequenced, and eukaryotic genomes contain many repeated sequences. Open crystal-structure databases consist of density-functional-theory calculations, each of which is computationally expensive, and the largest contain a few million structures. Appendix A describes each corpus.

3Measuring Data Efficiency

We measure every training method in units of unique data. Let $L_s(U)$ be the best validation loss that a single pass over $U$ unique tokens reaches, tuned over model size, learning rate and batch size. For a method that reaches loss $L(U)$ from the same $U$ tokens, the data multiplier is

$$m(U) = \frac{L_s^{-1}\big(L(U)\big)}{U},$$
(1)

the unique data a single pass would need to match the method, relative to $U$. We invert the frontier by log-linear interpolation between measured budgets (5M to 1.5B in chemistry). A multiplier of 6× at 50M means that the method trained on 50M unique tokens matches a single pass over 300M.

Every domain uses the same three references, each tuned on that domain's own data:

  • Single-epoch frontier. One pass over $U$ with a cosine schedule, best over model size, learning rate and batch size at each budget. It defines $L_s$ in Eq. 1.
  • Tuned multi-epoch. Plain repetition of the same $U$ tokens, tuned over model size, learning rate, weight decay, number of passes and batch size. It is the standard way to use repeated data.
  • Released models. Where a domain has published checkpoints trained on more data, we score them on our validation set and read off how many of their training tokens the recipe matches, interpolating log-linearly between their checkpoints (Section 8).

All multipliers measure unique-data efficiency, not compute efficiency. The recipe makes 128 passes over its data (6.4B token presentations at 50M unique tokens in chemistry). Looped depth increases computation per token, and pretraining evaluation averages 64 snapshots. The experiments do not establish a compute-matched advantage.

4The Recipe

The recipe combines three primitives from q0 and Chen et al., described below.

Snapshot ensembling. Training is split into cycles. Within each cycle the learning rate anneals linearly from its peak to $0.08\times$, while weight decay ramps from $0.7\times$ to $1\times$ in anti-phase. The weights at the end of each cycle are kept as a snapshot, and the final model averages the next-token probabilities of all $K$ snapshots:

$$p_{\text{ens}}(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{\theta_k}(x_t \mid x_{<t}).$$
(2)

Chain distillation. From the ninth cycle on, each cycle learns from the previous snapshot as well as from the data:

$$\mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{CE} + \alpha\,T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p^{(T)}_{\theta_{k-1}} \,\big\|\, p^{(T)}_{\theta}\big), \qquad \alpha = 0.45,\; T = 1.2.$$
(3)

After each snapshot the weights are perturbed per tensor, $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \sigma\,\mathrm{std}(\theta)\,z$ with $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and $\sigma$ decaying from 0.25 to 0.05, so consecutive snapshots start from different points.

Looped depth. One prelude block, ten core blocks applied three times with tied weights, and one coda block. Each loop boundary applies the boundary operator of Chen et al.: the state is RMS-normalised and a learned projection of the prelude output is re-injected. The model stores 85M parameters and executes 227M per token.

4.1Mixing Snapshots Across Runs

Snapshots from one run are highly correlated, so adding more of them to the ensemble gives diminishing returns (Section 6.1). Runs with different seeds or components are less correlated. The last step of the recipe pools the snapshots of every recipe run at a budget (the full recipe and its three ablations, all seeds) and combines their next-token probabilities with weights that adapt within each sequence:

$$w_{t+1} = (1-\alpha)\,\frac{w_t \odot p_t^{\beta}}{\lVert w_t \odot p_t^{\beta} \rVert_1} + \alpha\,\pi_{b(t)}, \qquad p_{\text{mix}}(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \sum_m w_{t,m}\, p_m(x_t \mid x_{<t}),$$
(4)

where $p_t$ holds each member's probability of the token just seen and $\pi_{b(t)}$ are weights fit by EM for each band of positions in the sequence. After each token, the weights of members that assigned it high probability increase, and the weights are then moved partly back toward the prior. The rule only uses earlier tokens of the same sequence, so the mixture is still a left-to-right model. The weights are never fit on the data they are scored on: the priors and $(\alpha, \beta)$ are fit on one half of the validation sequences and scored on the other half, then the other way round, and the two scores are averaged.

4.2Settings Across Budgets

We use one preset rule set rather than running a separate search for each budget. In chemistry every run trains an 85M model (12 layers, width 768) for exactly 128 passes over $U$, split into 64 cycles of two passes. Learning rate, batch size and weight decay follow preset scaling rules as $U$ changes; their exact values are in Appendix A.

5Pretraining Data Multipliers

We cut each budget $U$ from the start of a large training corpus, so the single-epoch frontier can be measured well beyond the largest budget. In chemistry the corpus is the SMILES collection of Xu et al., the budgets run from 5M to 200M unique tokens, and the frontier is measured to 1.5B; data, tokenisation, validation set and models are described in Appendix A.

The hyper-epoch recipe is worth 5.4–6.2× as much unique data at every budget (Figure 1), about twice the tuned multi-epoch arm (2.1–3.0×). Trained on 200M unique tokens it reaches 0.5676, which a single pass needs about 1.2B tokens to match. Unlike tuned multi-epoch training, its multiplier does not decline with $U$. Extra seeds move it by at most 0.11× at 5–50M and 0.25× at 100–200M, far less than the gaps between arms.

6Component Contributions

6.1Ensemble Size

The best single snapshot of a hyper-epoch run is worth 3.2–4.1×, already above tuned repetition. Averaging snapshots takes it to 5.4–6.2× (Figure 2). The two diverge with budget: the best single snapshot falls from 4.0× at 5M to 3.2× at 200M, while the full ensemble stays flat. At small budgets 16 snapshots recover almost all of the gain; at 150M and 200M the curve is still rising at 32–64, so larger budgets benefit from more snapshots.

6.2Leave-One-Out Ablations

At every budget we train the full recipe and three variants with everything else fixed: without distillation, without looping, and without both (cycles only). The run without ensembling is the best single snapshot of the full run. Cycles alone are worth 4.3–5.0×, still well above tuned repetition (Figure 3). Looping adds 0.3–1.3× and grows with budget: removing it costs 0.45× at 5M and 1.33× at 200M. Distillation adds 0.4–0.6× at $U \le 50$M and 0.04–0.17× from 100M on.

Fine-tuning shows the same pattern with more noise (Figure 4). Removing ensembling hurts at every budget. Removing looping is neutral below 50M and clearly harmful from 100M on. Removing distillation costs little at 20–50M and more at the extremes. No component can be dropped across the whole range without a measurable loss, although at $U \le 20$M the run without looping fine-tunes as well as the full recipe.

7Property Prediction

7.1Protocol

We test whether lower pretraining loss leads to better property prediction. We fine-tune on eight MoleculeNet tasks (Wu et al.) used by Xu et al.: BACE, BBBP, ClinTox, SIDER and Tox21 (ROC-AUC) and ESOL, FreeSolv and Lipophilicity (RMSE). HIV is left out for cost. We use ten scaffold splits (80/10/10) rather than random splits, which saturate and would hide differences between models.

The pretrained network is frozen and we train rank-8 LoRA adapters (Hu et al.) on the attention query and value projections plus a two-layer head, with batch 64, weight decay 0.01, EMA weights and early stopping; the learning rate is chosen per task on validation. Test-time averaging is equal across arms: every reported model averages 16 sets of predictions, from 16 snapshots for an ensemble and from 16 fine-tuning seeds for a single checkpoint.

To summarise eight tasks in one number, we fit each task's single-epoch score log-linearly in $U$ and pool the tasks with inverse-variance weights. The pooled score is in units of $\ln U$ on the single-epoch curve, so one unit is a factor of $e$ in data. Individual task scores are reported in Appendix C.

7.2Results

The pretraining gain is still present after fine-tuning (Figure 5). At 50M unique pretraining tokens, the hyper-epoch model scores +7.69 on the pooled measure, above our single-epoch 3B model at +7.35. At 100M it reaches +8.27, above the best released Xu et al. 3B-token checkpoint at +7.97. At 200M it reaches +8.61. The corresponding fine-tuning data multipliers are shown in Appendix B; the direct score comparison is easier to interpret because the single-epoch downstream curve is relatively flat.

The pooled result is supported by individual tasks: the recipe beats our single-epoch 3B model on four of eight tasks at 50M unique tokens and seven of eight at 200M. The full breakdown, including the comparison with the released Xu et al. checkpoint, is in Appendix C.

8Comparison with Released Models

We scored the released checkpoints of Xu et al. (one pass over 100M, 300M, 1B and 3B tokens, best model size at each) on our validation set and fine-tuned them with the same 16-seed protocol. Comparing them with the hyper-epoch recipe mixes two differences, which we report separately (Figure 6).

Effect 1: our single pass against theirs. Both are single-epoch models, so this isolates the training setup. In pretraining loss our frontier is better at every size: it matches their 100M, 300M, 1B and 3B models with about 35M, 70M, 210M and 490M tokens. In fine-tuning the order reverses at equal data: their 3B model scores +7.97, our single pass over 3B +7.35. Their training code applies dropout 0.1 during pretraining and ours does not; dropout would raise single-pass loss while possibly helping transfer. We have not yet tested which difference accounts for the gap.

Effect 2: hyper-epoch training against our single pass. This is the subject of the post and is measured entirely against our own frontier. The recipe reaches the loss of their 3B model (0.5735) at about 85M unique tokens, 5.7× less than our single pass needs. On fine-tuning, where Effect 1 goes the other way, the recipe still scores above their 3B model from 100M unique tokens.

Both effects together. For each budget we read off how many tokens a single pass of Xu et al. needs to reach the recipe's pretraining loss, interpolating log-linearly in tokens between their best checkpoints at 100M, 300M, 1B and 3B (Table 3). Trained on 10M–50M unique tokens, the recipe matches their 160M-token to 1.4B-token models, 16–28× more data. From 100M unique tokens on, its loss is lower than that of their best 3B-token model; extending their last segment puts the equivalent at 3.9B–6.2B tokens, 31–39× the data. Across the whole range the recipe needs 13–39× less unique data than the best released models.

9Conclusion and Open Questions

In the SMILES corpus we measured, new structure becomes rare long before the supply of molecules is exhausted. With a fixed SMILES corpus, plain repetition is worth two to three times the unique data. One shared hyper-epoch recipe of ensembling, chain distillation and looping is worth five to six times, at every budget from 5M to 200M unique tokens, and reaches the pretraining loss of the best released SMILES models with 13–39× less unique data. The benefit transfers to property prediction: trained on 50M unique tokens the recipe fine-tunes better than a single pass over 3B, and at 100M it beats the best released 3B-token model. Each component contributes over part of the range, and ensembling contributes everywhere.

Three questions remain open. First, does the multiplier keep holding above 200M unique tokens, where the single-epoch frontier itself becomes capacity-limited? Second, why does looping help more as data grows? Chen et al. find that in multi-epoch training looping acts as a regulariser and that it becomes compute-optimal to add loops as models grow, which suggests the loop count should scale with the budget too. Third, does the recipe hold for continuous prediction objectives? Every domain in this post is trained by next-token prediction over a discrete vocabulary, and each component relies on that: snapshot ensembling and the mixture average categorical next-token distributions, and chain distillation matches them with a KL divergence. Objectives such as regressing atomic coordinates, energies or forces, or training diffusion and flow-matching models, have no categorical output to average, so each component would need a counterpart for continuous outputs. We have not tested whether the multipliers carry over to that setting.

10Related Work

Data-constrained training. Muennighoff et al. fit scaling laws for repeated data and found that the value of repeats decays after a few epochs. Baron et al. showed that dense models with Muon and strong weight decay substantially improve multi-epoch training for proteins, and named snapshot ensembling and chain distillation as next steps, which this work takes up. For web text, Kim, K. et al. show that with ample compute, much stronger regularisation, ensembling and distillation reach the same loss with 5.17× less data at 200M tokens.

Hyper-epoch primitives. Snapshot ensembles (Huang et al.) reuse a cyclic schedule (Loshchilov and Hutter) to collect many models from one run. Hinton et al. and Furlanello et al. studied distilling a model into a fresh copy of itself. q0 combines these into primitives for hyper-epoch pretraining.

Looped transformers. Weight-tied depth dates to the Universal Transformer (Dehghani et al.). Chen et al. introduce the boundary operator we use and show that recursion and model growth change pretraining scaling exponents.

Chemical data and diversity. PubChem (Kim, S. et al.) is the largest open database of known compounds, and make-on-demand spaces such as Enamine REAL (Enamine) enumerate many more from a fixed set of building blocks and reactions. Bemis–Murcko scaffolds (Bemis and Murcko) are the standard way to group molecules by framework and underlie the scaffold splits we use.

Chemical language models. Xu et al. study scaling of SMILES language models and release the checkpoints we compare against. MoleculeNet (Wu et al.) provides the downstream tasks.

Appendix

ADomain Details

Chemistry (SMILES).

We pretrain on the SMILES corpora of Xu et al. with their 591-token vocabulary. Every run is scored on the same held-out set, the first 2M tokens of the SMILES-100M validation split, by next-token cross-entropy in nats per token. Whole molecules are packed into 8,192-token rows with a block-diagonal attention mask, so the unique-token count $U$ is exact. Models are pre-LN decoder-only transformers with rotary embeddings and no dropout, on the width and depth ladder of Xu et al. (1.3M to 278M non-embedding parameters). All runs use Muon for matrix parameters and AdamW for embeddings and the output head, with no warmup.

The two tuned reference arms in chemistry:

  • Single-epoch frontier. One pass over $U \in \{5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1500\}$M tokens with a cosine schedule and no weight decay, tuned over model size, learning rate and batch size (16k to 262k tokens). A single pass over 3B tokens with the 278M model reaches 0.568, worse than 1.5B (0.5665): the ladder is capacity-limited there, so the 3B run serves as a fine-tuning reference but not as a frontier point.
  • Tuned multi-epoch. Repeated passes over $U \in \{5, \dots, 200\}$M at a constant learning rate, re-packing every pass and stopping early, tuned over model size, learning rate, weight decay, number of passes and batch size.
