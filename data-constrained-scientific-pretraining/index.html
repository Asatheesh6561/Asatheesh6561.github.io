Q Labs Research · Applications, Part 1

Data-Efficient Scientific Pretraining

One training recipe makes scarce scientific data go further across molecules, DNA, and crystal structures.

November 2026

TL;DR

  • The question: how much can a model learn from a fixed scientific corpus? We test one repeated-data recipe on molecules, eukaryotic DNA, and crystal structures.
  • The result: against a tuned single pass, one run is worth 5.4–6.2× as much unique data for molecules, 5.5–8.9× for DNA, and 7.4–13.3× for crystals at budgets where the multiplier can be measured. Tuned plain repetition is worth 2.1–5.7× across the three domains.
  • The comparison: on the same data, the recipe needs about 20× less data than the published training recipes for DNA and crystals. For molecules, 85M unique tokens reach the loss of Xu et al.'s best released model, trained on 4.4B tokens.
  • The transfer: gains extend to property prediction, gene annotation, clinical variant effects, and zero-shot crystal structure prediction. Models trained on 25–50M unique tokens fine-tune as well as or better than the longest single pass in each domain.

1Introduction

Compute for training models keeps getting cheaper. Data in the sciences does not. A computed crystal structure costs a density-functional calculation and an experimental one a synthesis. The part of a eukaryotic genome that carries most of the learnable signal is small and fixed by biology: OpenGenome2's entire curated set of eukaryotic genic windows is 0.42 trillion bases, and for most lineages far less exists. As compute outgrows data, the useful question shifts from how to get more data to how much a model can extract from the data it has.

For web text, Muennighoff et al. found that a few repeated epochs are almost as good as new data and that each further repeat is worth quickly less. Our work on protein language models (Baron et al.) showed that a tuned multi-epoch recipe goes much further. q0 then introduced hyper-epoch training, primitives built for many passes over the same data.

Here we ask whether one such recipe works across science, not in one carefully chosen domain. We picked three domains that differ in almost every way a sequence model can see: SMILES strings of molecules (a 591-token vocabulary, short documents), eukaryotic DNA (8 tokens, 2,046-base windows) and crystal structures written as CIF files (371 tokens, numbers and symmetry symbols). In each we build the same measurement from scratch: a tuned single-epoch frontier, a tuned multi-epoch baseline, the domain's published training recipe, and downstream tasks where pretraining is known to matter.

Our contributions:

  • One recipe that, in all three domains, makes one training run worth 5.4–13.3× as much unique data as a tuned single pass, well above what tuned repetition achieves in the same domain (2.1–5.7×).
  • Gains of about 20× against the published training recipes for DNA (Evo 2) and crystals (CrystaLLM), run on the same data.
  • Downstream evidence in every domain, with LoRA fine-tuning and zero-shot evaluations on held-out data.
  • Leave-one-out ablations in every domain, which separate the components that help everywhere from those that help in one domain only.

2Method

2.1Measuring Data Efficiency

We measure every method in units of unique data. Let $L_s(U)$ be the best held-out loss that a single pass over $U$ unique tokens reaches, tuned over model size, learning rate and batch size. For a method that reaches loss $L(U)$ from the same $U$ tokens, the data multiplier is

$$m(U) = \frac{L_s^{-1}\big(L(U)\big)}{U},$$
(1)

the unique data a single pass would need to match the method, relative to $U$. We invert the frontier by log-linear interpolation between measured budgets. When a method beats a single pass over the whole corpus, we report only the lower bound (corpus size / $U$). A multiplier of 10× at 25M means that 25M tokens trained this way are worth 250M tokens seen once. Downstream multipliers are defined the same way on the downstream score of single-epoch models.

Each domain is scored on data no model trained on: unseen molecules for SMILES, OpenGenome2's own held-out split for DNA (with every choice made on a separate set of held-out genera), and held-out CIFs with structural duplicates of training CIFs removed for crystals.

2.2The Recipe

The recipe takes its components from q0 and Chen et al. (Figure 1). Two are used in every domain:

Snapshot ensembling. Training is split into 64 cycles. Within each cycle the learning rate anneals linearly from its peak to $0.08\times$, while weight decay ramps from $0.7\times$ to $1\times$ in anti-phase. The weights at the end of each cycle are kept as a snapshot, and the model averages the next-token probabilities of its snapshots:

$$p_{\text{ens}}(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{\theta_k}(x_t \mid x_{<t}).$$
(2)

Looped depth. One prelude block, a core of blocks applied several times with tied weights (three times for molecules and crystals, twice for DNA), and one coda block. Each loop boundary applies the boundary operator of Chen et al.: the state is RMS-normalised and a learned projection of the prelude output is re-injected.

Two further components were tested everywhere and kept only where they helped:

Chain distillation trains each cycle on the previous snapshot's predictions as well as the data, $\mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{CE} + \alpha\,T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p^{(T)}_{\theta_{k-1}} \,\|\, p^{(T)}_{\theta}\big)$. Weight perturbation adds $\sigma\,\mathrm{std}(\theta)\,z$ to each tensor after a snapshot, so the next cycle starts from a different point.

2.3What Is Tuned

The cycle shape, the 64 snapshots and the loop count are fixed defaults carried from molecules to the other domains. Per domain and budget we tune only the model size, the number of passes, the peak learning rate and the weight decay (Appendix C).

3Three Domains

Each domain has its own corpus, held-out set, downstream tasks and published baseline (Table 2). All models are decoder-only transformers trained from scratch with Muon for matrix parameters and AdamW for embeddings and the output head, with no warmup and no dropout, on whole documents packed with a document mask so that the unique-token count is exact.

4Pretraining

In every domain one run of the recipe sits well below the single-epoch frontier and below tuned repetition (Figure 2). For molecules it is worth 5.4–6.2× as much unique data at every budget from 5M to 200M. For DNA it is worth 5.5–8.9× on the held-out split, rising from 5M to 20M and then holding at 8–9×. For crystals it is worth 7.4–13.3× at 5M–50M, and from 100M on it beats a single pass over the whole corpus, so only lower bounds are measured there (>6.9× and >3.5×).

Figure 3 puts the three domains on one axis. Tuned repetition declines with budget in every domain, while the recipe holds or rises. The multipliers differ between domains mostly because the frontiers differ: on the crystal frontier each doubling of data buys only 0.014–0.025 nats, so the recipe's nearly constant gain of 0.055–0.065 nats is worth many doublings; the DNA frontier sits on a plateau until about 50M tokens and then drops sharply.

Every number in the main text is for one training run. With more compute, training several runs at a budget and mixing them raises the multipliers further (Appendix B).

5Against Published Recipes

Each domain has a published model whose training recipe we can run or score. These comparisons mix two effects: our tuned single pass against theirs, and repetition against a single pass. Only the second is the value of repeated data, so we report both (Table 4).

DNA. Evo 2's recipe (one pass, AdamW with warmup and cosine decay) on a parameter-matched scaled-down Evo 2 is worth only 0.2–1.7× our single pass: it stays on a loss plateau until about 300M tokens. On its curve one run of our recipe needs 18–24× less data from 5M to 200M, and at 100M unique tokens it matches the held-out loss the Evo 2 recipe reaches with 2B. Both the optimiser and the architecture contribute to the plateau: the scaled-down Evo 2 trained with our recipe is as good as our transformer.

Crystals. CrystaLLM's recipe, tuned per budget for one pass, is worth 0.3–0.6× our single pass. On its curve one run of our recipe is worth 21–24× at 5M–25M, and from 50M on it is below that recipe's loss on the whole corpus. CrystaLLM's released checkpoints cannot serve as baselines: they were trained on all of CrystaLLM's data, including the held-out split.

Molecules. Xu et al. released single-pass checkpoints trained on up to 4.4B tokens. Our tuned single pass reaches the loss of their best model with about 9× less data, and the recipe needs a further 5.7×: it reaches that loss at about 85M unique tokens. In fine-tuning, their best model is ahead of our single pass over 3B, but the recipe overtakes it from 100M unique tokens.

6Downstream Tasks

Lower pretraining loss matters only if it transfers. In each domain we chose downstream tasks where pretraining is known to help, and we fine-tune with LoRA adapters on a frozen network so that the comparison is about the pretrained representation (Figure 4).

  • Molecules: eight MoleculeNet tasks under scaffold splits, pooled into one score in units of $\ln U$ on the single-epoch curve. From 50M unique tokens the recipe fine-tunes better than a single pass over 3B tokens.
  • DNA: per-base gene annotation (intergenic, intron, exon, coding) in six held-out genera. One run of the recipe, with its fine-tuned snapshots averaged, is worth 8.2–10.7× at 5M–20M and beats a single pass over 2B from 50M on. The scaled-down Evo 2 trained the Evo 2 way levels off and falls below our single pass from 200M.
  • Crystals: three Matbench property tasks (perovskite formation energy, shear and bulk modulus). One fine-tuned snapshot of the recipe is worth 11.1× and 7.0× at 5M and 10M, and from 25M on it fine-tunes as well as or better than a single pass over the whole corpus.

Zero-shot evaluations tell the same story (Table 5). For DNA, our recipe at 50M–200M unique tokens predicts the effect of human ClinVar variants, a species absent from our training data, as well as single-epoch models trained on 13–14× more data; across all models, ClinVar AUROC follows pretraining loss. For crystals, prompted with a composition and sampling one structure, the recipe at 5M–50M matches the true structure as often as single-epoch models trained on 4.9–10.5× more data and CrystaLLM's recipe trained on 15–24× more.

7What Makes It Work

At each budget we retrain the best configuration with one component removed; the run without ensembling is the best single snapshot of the full run (Figure 5).

  • Ensembling helps everywhere. The best single snapshot is worth 3.2–4.1× for molecules and 5.0–9.9× for crystals at 5M–50M, against 5.4–6.2× and 7.4–13.3× for the snapshot average. For DNA it adds 1–2× at every budget except 10M, where the arms are within seed noise.
  • Looping helps everywhere, and more as data grows. Removing it costs molecules 0.45× at 5M and 1.33× at 200M, and DNA 0.9× at 20M and 3.4× at 200M. For crystals it helps at every budget (0.8–2.3×).
  • The extras are domain-specific. Chain distillation adds 0.4–0.6× for molecules at 50M and below, little for DNA, and nothing for crystals. Weight perturbation helps measurably only for crystals at 10M.

8Conclusion

In three scientific domains that share almost nothing at the level of tokens, one recipe of snapshot ensembling and looped depth makes a training run worth five to thirteen times its unique data, well above tuned repetition, and needs about twenty times less data than the published training recipes on DNA and crystals. The gain transfers to every downstream task we measured, and in every domain the recipe trained on tens of millions of unique tokens fine-tunes as well as or better than our longest single pass.

Three questions remain open. First, how far does the multiplier hold beyond 200M unique tokens, where single-epoch frontiers become capacity-limited and multipliers turn into lower bounds? Second, why does looping help more as data grows? Chen et al. find that in multi-epoch training looping acts as a regulariser and that adding loops becomes compute-optimal as models grow, which suggests scaling the loop count with the budget. Third, which further domains does the recipe transfer to unchanged? We will report more domains in the next parts of this series.

9Related Work

Data-constrained training. Muennighoff et al. fit scaling laws for repeated data. Baron et al. showed that dense models with Muon and strong weight decay push multi-epoch training much further for proteins, and named snapshot ensembling and chain distillation as next steps.

Hyper-epoch primitives. Snapshot ensembles (Huang et al.) reuse a cyclic schedule (Loshchilov and Hutter) to collect many models from one run. Distilling a model into a fresh copy of itself goes back to Hinton et al. and Furlanello et al.. q0 combines these into primitives for hyper-epoch pretraining. Weight-tied depth dates to the Universal Transformer (Dehghani et al.); Chen et al. introduce the boundary operator we use.

Scientific language models. Xu et al. study scaling of SMILES language models. Evo 2 (Brixi et al.) is trained on OpenGenome2, the source of our DNA. CrystaLLM (Antunes et al.) generates crystal structures as CIF text. Our downstream tasks come from MoleculeNet (Wu et al.), Matbench (Dunn et al.) and ClinVar (Landrum et al.).

Appendix

AFull Pretraining Results

BMore Compute, More Gain: Mixing Runs

Everything in the main text is one training run, so its cost is one run. If more compute is available, the same data can be pushed further: train several runs at a budget and combine them into one predictor. The snapshots of one run are close to each other. Separate runs, with different seeds, weight decay, model shapes, numbers of passes or recipe components, make more different errors, and a mixture can exploit that.

Adaptive mixture. Every second (DNA) or fourth (crystals) snapshot of every run at a budget becomes a member $j$ with predictive distribution $p_j$. The mixture predicts $p(x_t) = \sum_j w_{t,j}\, p_j(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. Its weights start from a prior $\pi$ fitted with EM and are updated within each document after every token:

$$w_{t+1,j} = (1-\alpha)\,\frac{w_{t,j}\,p_j(x_t \mid x_{<t})^{\beta}}{\sum_i w_{t,i}\,p_i(x_t \mid x_{<t})^{\beta}} + \alpha\,\pi_j,$$
(B1)

so members that predict the current document well gain weight, while a fraction $\alpha$ is always pulled back toward the prior. The prior and $(\beta, \alpha)$ are fitted on data that is never scored: on the validation genera for DNA, and by cross-fitting on halves of the held-out CIFs (fit on one half, score the other, both ways round) for crystals. With $\beta = 0$ the mixture is a plain weighted ensemble of runs ("fixed weights").

How much more it buys depends on how different the runs are. For DNA, mixing 8–11 runs lifts the multiplier from 5.5–8.9× to 10.0–14.0×, and to 25–31× on the Evo 2 recipe's curve. Most of the gain over the best run comes from the within-document reweighting: with fixed weights the mixture is worth 8.7–10.4×. At 5M–20M even three seeds of any single arm reach 8.4–10.4×; from 50M on, mixtures of looped runs are clearly better than mixtures of non-looped ones. For crystals the mixture adds only 0.7–1.4× at 5M–25M and nothing at 50M, although it pools 28–91 runs. Nearly all of those runs have the same tuned shape and make much the same errors. On CrystaLLM's recipe curve the crystal mixture is worth 24.5–25.7× at 5M–25M. We have not run the cross-fitted mixture for molecules.

Cost. A mixture costs the training compute of all its runs, and scoring it means evaluating every member on every token. The gain per extra run falls quickly once the runs stop differing. If compute is spent on more runs, it should go to runs that differ (other seeds, weight decays, shapes, recipe components) rather than to copies of the best configuration.

CTraining Details

The cycle shape (learning rate from its peak to $0.08\times$, weight decay from $0.7\times$ to $1\times$ in anti-phase), the 64 snapshots and the loop count are fixed defaults carried from molecules. Per domain and budget we tune the model size, the number of passes, the peak learning rate and the weight decay. The product of learning rate and weight decay matters most: when it is too small the snapshots memorise the training data, and its optimum falls as the budget grows.

  • Molecules. An 85M model (12 layers, width 768) for 128 passes, all of them in 64 two-pass cycles. Peak Muon learning rate 0.02 for $U \le 50$M and 0.01 above (AdamW 0.002 for embeddings and the head); batch 16k tokens for $U \le 20$M and 65k above. Weight decay follows $\mathrm{lr}\cdot\mathrm{wd}\cdot(\text{steps per pass}) \approx 1.22$, a weight-decay timescale of about 0.82 passes at every budget. Chain distillation from cycle 9 ($\alpha = 0.45$, $T = 1.2$) with weight perturbation ($\sigma$ from 0.25 to 0.05).
  • DNA. A flat learning-rate phase over the first half of training, then 64 cycles; the ensemble is the best prefix of snapshots, chosen on validation. The recipe was assembled one component at a time at 5M and 10M on a 25M model, then carried to larger budgets on an 85M model with a short learning-rate × weight-decay bracket per budget. Core looped twice; chain distillation from cycle 32 ($\alpha = 0.2$ at 5M, 0.7 above). Weight perturbation and distillation from the running ensemble gave nothing.
  • Crystals. A 113M model (1 prelude, 10 core blocks applied three times, 1 coda; about 300M executed parameters per token), batch 16 rows of 1,024 tokens, a flat phase over the first half then 64 cycles, weight perturbation after each snapshot. The number of passes (8–192), learning rate and weight decay were tuned per budget, 52–224 runs per budget. The optimal product of learning rate and weight decay falls from $2\cdot10^{-3}$ at 5M to $3\cdot10^{-5}$ at 200M, and at 5M–10M half the optimum lets the snapshots memorise. Precycle fraction, cycle floors, snapshot count, loop count and perturbation size, each varied once around the defaults, moved the loss by less than 0.0005 except a larger perturbation, which hurt.

All models are decoder-only transformers trained from scratch with Muon for matrix parameters and AdamW for embeddings and the output head, no warmup and no dropout. Documents are packed whole with a document mask, so the unique-token count $U$ is exact.
