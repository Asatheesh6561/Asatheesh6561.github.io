Data-Efficient Scientific Pretraining

November 2026

TL;DR

  • We present one pretraining recipe for repeated data that works across three scientific domains which share almost nothing at the level of tokens: molecules as SMILES strings, eukaryotic DNA, and crystal structures as CIF files. The recipe combines snapshot ensembling over cyclic schedules with looped depth.
  • One training run is worth 5.4–6.2× as much unique data as a tuned single pass for molecules, 5.5–8.9× for DNA, and 7.4–13.3× for crystals where the full multiplier can be measured. Tuned plain repetition is worth only 2.1–3.0×, 2.9–5.7× and 3.3–4.4×.
  • Against the published training recipe of each domain, run on the same data, the recipe needs about 20× less data for DNA (Evo 2) and crystals (CrystaLLM). For molecules, 85M unique tokens reach the loss of Xu et al.'s best released model, which was trained on 4.4B tokens.
  • The gains carry over beyond pretraining, to molecular and materials property prediction, gene annotation, the GUE DNA benchmarks, zero-shot clinical variant effects and zero-shot crystal structure prediction. With LoRA fine-tuning on the 27 GUE benchmarks, one run is worth 2.8–15.5× as much unique data as a single pass.
  • Snapshot ensembling and looping help in every domain, and looping matters more as the dataset grows. Chain distillation and weight perturbation help only in some domains.

1Introduction

Language models for science are increasingly limited by data rather than compute. Training compute keeps getting cheaper, while new scientific data does not. A computed crystal structure costs a density-functional calculation and an experimental one a synthesis and a refinement. The informative part of a genome is fixed by biology: OpenGenome2's entire curated set of eukaryotic genic windows contains 0.42 trillion bases, and for most lineages far less exists. When a dataset cannot grow, the question becomes how much a model can learn from each example it already has.

The usual answer is to repeat the data. For web text, Muennighoff et al. found that the first few repeated epochs are almost as good as new data and that later ones add little. Our work on protein language models (Baron et al.) showed that a carefully tuned multi-epoch setup goes much further, and q0 introduced training primitives designed for many passes over the same data.

In this work, we ask whether one such recipe transfers across science, rather than working in a single carefully chosen domain. We pick three domains that differ in almost every way a sequence model can see: SMILES strings of molecules with a 591-token vocabulary, eukaryotic DNA with 8 tokens and 2,046-base windows, and crystal structures written as CIF files with 371 tokens, mostly numbers and symmetry symbols. In each domain we build the same measurement from scratch: a tuned single-pass frontier, tuned plain repetition, the domain's published training recipe, and downstream tasks where pretraining is known to matter.

We make the following contributions.

  • We present one repeated-data recipe, snapshot ensembling with looped depth, that in all three domains makes one training run worth 5.4–13.3× as much unique data as a tuned single pass, well above what tuned repetition achieves in the same domain (2.1–5.7×).
  • Against the published training recipes for DNA and crystals, run on the same data, the recipe needs about 20× less data. For molecules it reaches the loss of Xu et al.'s best released model with 85M unique tokens instead of 4.4B.
  • The gain transfers to downstream tasks in every domain, with LoRA fine-tuning and zero-shot, including the GUE DNA benchmarks.
  • Leave-one-out ablations in every domain separate the components that help everywhere, snapshot ensembling and looping, from those that help in one domain only.

The goal of this work is to establish a strong default for pretraining on scarce scientific data, measured in units of data rather than loss. Beyond learning rate, weight decay, model size and the number of passes, we do not tune the recipe separately for each domain. We also do not study budgets beyond 200M unique tokens, where single-pass baselines become hard to measure and many of our multipliers turn into lower bounds.

2Method

Our recipe works as follows. We train on a fixed set of $U$ unique tokens for many passes. Training is split into cycles that each end in a snapshot of the weights, and at prediction time the model averages the snapshots' next-token probabilities. The model itself is looped: a core of blocks is applied several times with shared weights, which adds computation per token without adding parameters that can memorise the training data. Two further components are kept only in the domains where they help.

2.1Snapshot Ensembling

A single model trained for many passes eventually memorises its training data. Snapshot ensembling instead collects many models from one run (Huang et al.). We divide training into 64 cycles and save the weights at the end of each one. Within a cycle, the learning rate falls linearly from its peak to $0.08\times$ while weight decay rises from $0.7\times$ to $1\times$ in anti-phase, so each cycle ends in a well-converged snapshot and the next one moves away from it. At prediction time we average the $K$ snapshots' next-token probabilities:

$$p_{\text{ens}}(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{\theta_k}(x_t \mid x_{<t}).$$
(1)

For molecules the cycles span all of training; for DNA and crystals they follow a flat learning-rate phase over the first half of training.

2.2Looped Depth

The model has a prelude block, a core of blocks whose weights are reused, and a coda block. We apply the core two or three times. At each loop boundary, the boundary operator of Chen et al. normalises the state and re-injects a learned projection of the prelude's output. The 113M crystal model, for example, executes about 300M parameters per token.

2.3Domain-Specific Components

We tested two further components in every domain and kept them only where they helped. Chain distillation trains each new cycle on the previous snapshot's predictions as well as on the data, $\mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{CE} + \alpha\,T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p^{(T)}_{\theta_{k-1}} \,\|\, p^{(T)}_{\theta}\big)$. Weight perturbation gives the model a small random push after each snapshot, $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \sigma\,\mathrm{std}(\theta)\,z$, so that the next cycle starts from a different point. Appendix C describes where each one is used.

3Pretraining With Repeated Scientific Data

3.1Setup

We train decoder-only transformers from scratch in each domain (Table 1). Documents are packed whole with a document mask, so the number of unique training tokens $U$ is exact. All models use Muon for matrix parameters and AdamW for embeddings and the output head, without warmup or dropout.

We compare against three baselines in every domain. The single-epoch frontier is one pass over $U$ unique tokens, tuned over model size, learning rate and batch size at every budget. Tuned repetition makes several passes over the same data and is tuned over model size, learning rate, weight decay and the number of passes. The published recipe is the training recipe of the domain's best-known model: Xu et al.'s released molecular checkpoints, Evo 2's recipe on a parameter-matched scaled-down Evo 2, and CrystaLLM's recipe, the last two trained by us on the same data streams.

We measure every method in units of unique data. Let $L_s(U)$ be the frontier's best validation loss after one pass over $U$ unique tokens. If a method reaches loss $L(U)$ from the same $U$ tokens, its data multiplier is

$$m(U) = \frac{L_s^{-1}\big(L(U)\big)}{U},$$
(2)

the unique data a single pass would need to match the method, relative to $U$. A multiplier of 10× at 25M means that 25M tokens trained this way are worth 250M tokens seen once. We invert the frontier by interpolating linearly in the logarithm of $U$ between measured budgets. When a result is better than the longest measured single pass, we extend the frontier's final segment; these extrapolated multipliers depend on its final slope and are marked † throughout. Downstream multipliers are defined the same way on downstream scores.

All scores use held-out data: unseen molecules, OpenGenome2's own held-out DNA split, and held-out crystal structures with structural duplicates of training structures removed. For DNA, every model choice is made on a separate set of held-out genera. Per domain and budget, we tune the model size, the number of passes, the peak learning rate and the weight decay of the recipe; the cycle shape, the 64 snapshots and the loop count are fixed (Appendix C).

3.2Main Results

In all three domains, one run of the recipe reaches a lower validation loss than both the tuned single pass and tuned repetition at every budget (Figure 1). For molecules it is worth 5.4–6.2× the unique data from 5M to 200M tokens. For DNA it is worth 5.5–8.9× on the held-out split, rising through 20M and then holding near 8–9×. For crystals it is worth 7.4–13.3× from 5M to 50M; at 100M and 200M it beats a single pass over the entire available corpus, and extending the frontier gives 8.2×† and 5.8×†.

Tuned repetition loses value as the dataset grows for molecules and DNA and stays at 3.3–4.4× for crystals, while the recipe keeps its advantage or improves on it. How large the multiplier is depends mostly on how quickly a single pass improves with more data. On the crystal frontier, each doubling of the data lowers the loss by only 0.014–0.025 nats, so the recipe's nearly constant gain of 0.053–0.064 nats is worth several doublings. The DNA frontier behaves differently: it stays nearly flat until about 50M tokens and then improves sharply.

All multipliers are for one training run per budget; combining several runs improves them further at extra compute cost (Appendix B).

3.3Comparison With Published Recipes

Each domain has a published model whose training recipe we can run or score. These comparisons mix two effects: the strength of our tuned single pass against theirs, and the value of repetition against a single pass. Only the second is the subject of this work, so we report both.

For DNA, we train a parameter-matched scaled-down Evo 2 for one pass with Evo 2's recipe: AdamW, warmup and cosine decay. Its loss barely moves until about 300M tokens, and its single pass is worth 0.2–1.7× ours. On its curve, one run of our recipe needs 18–24× less data from 5M to 200M, and at 100M unique tokens it matches the loss the Evo 2 recipe reaches with 2B. Both the optimiser and the architecture contribute to the plateau: trained with our recipe, the scaled-down Evo 2 is as good as our transformer. Fine-tuned on the GUE benchmarks, Evo 2's recipe is ahead of ours at 5M and level at 10M, and ours leads from 20M on (Section 4.3).

For crystals, we tune CrystaLLM's one-pass recipe at every budget. It is worth 0.3–0.6× our tuned single pass. On its curve, our recipe is worth 21–24× at 5M–25M, and from 50M on it beats CrystaLLM's one-pass result on the whole corpus. We train these baselines ourselves because CrystaLLM's released checkpoints saw data from the held-out split. Fine-tuned on Matbench, CrystaLLM's recipe is worse than our single pass on 31 of 32 task–budget pairs (Section 4.4).

For molecules, Xu et al. released single-pass checkpoints trained on up to 4.4B tokens. Our tuned single pass matches their best model's loss with about 9× less data, and the recipe needs a further 5.7× less, reaching the same loss with about 85M unique tokens. In property prediction, the recipe overtakes Xu et al.'s best model from 100M unique tokens.

4Downstream Transfer

A lower pretraining loss is only useful if it helps on real tasks. We evaluate transfer in two ways: by using the pretrained model directly in zero-shot tests, and by fine-tuning it on a task.

4.1Zero-shot Evaluation

Zero-shot tests use the pretrained models without any task-specific training (Figure 2, Tables 2 and 3). Each is compared with our single pass and with the published recipe of its domain, run on the same data.

For DNA, we score human ClinVar variants by the model's log-likelihood of the alternative sequence relative to the reference. Models trained with our recipe on 50M–200M unique tokens match single-pass models trained on 13–14× more data. Evo 2's recipe on the scaled-down Evo 2 does worse than our single pass: it stays near chance until 500M tokens and reaches 0.638 at 2B. Our recipe passes that from 50M unique tokens, more than 40× less data, and at 20M it matches the Evo 2 recipe at about 0.9B. Humans are absent from our training set, and across models the ClinVar score tracks pretraining loss.

For crystals, we prompt the model with a composition and ask it to generate a structure. At 5M–50M unique tokens, the recipe matches the true structure as often as a single pass trained on 4.9–10.5× more data, or CrystaLLM's recipe trained on 15–24× more.

All fine-tuning in the main text uses small LoRA adapters on a frozen pretrained network, so a task's score reflects what pretraining put into the representation. At each budget we fine-tune checkpoints from the recipe, the single-epoch frontier and the domain's published recipe with the same protocol, and report each domain separately (Figures 3–5).

4.2Fine-tuning: Molecules

BACE, BBBP, ClinTox, SIDER and Tox21 are classification benchmarks scored by ROC-AUC, and ESOL, FreeSolv and Lipophilicity are regression benchmarks scored by RMSE, all on scaffold splits. Pooled over the eight tasks, the recipe trained on 50M unique tokens fine-tunes better than a single pass over 3B tokens, and from 100M it overtakes the best released model of Xu et al. The individual curves show how the gain varies between tasks.

4.3Fine-tuning: DNA

Gene annotation labels each base of a 2,046-base window as intergenic, intronic, non-coding exonic or coding, in six genera that no model trained on. At 5M–20M tokens, the recipe's averaged fine-tuned snapshots are worth 8.2–10.7× as much unique data, and from 50M on they beat a single pass over 2B tokens.

We also fine-tune on all 27 eukaryotic tasks of the GUE suite (Zhou et al.): histone marks in yeast, transcription-factor binding in human and mouse, promoter detection and splice-site prediction. Each checkpoint is fine-tuned with the gene-annotation protocol, the learning rate is chosen by validation MCC, and we report test MCC. A randomly initialised network under the same adapters is the no-pretraining control. Averaged over the 27 tasks, the recipe is worth 2.8–15.5× as much unique data as a single pass (Table 4), and at 200M it comes within 0.01 MCC of a single pass over 2B tokens. Against Evo 2's recipe on the scaled-down Evo 2 it is behind at 5M, level at 10M and ahead from 20M on.

We leave out the 18 Nucleotide Transformer tasks. On the six we ran in full, a single pass gains only 0.06 MCC between 5M and 2B unique tokens, too little to measure a data multiplier; the recipe is ahead of it at five of six budgets, by at most 0.015 MCC. The results use one fine-tuning seed and one recipe run per budget, and single-task differences under about 0.02 MCC are within noise.

4.4Fine-tuning: Crystals

The Matbench tasks are perovskite formation energy, log shear modulus, log bulk modulus and phonons, each scored by MAE. On perovskites and the two moduli, one fine-tuned snapshot of the recipe beats the single pass at the same budget at every budget; phonons, with only 1,265 structures, gains from 25M on. At 50M unique tokens the recipe beats both a single pass and CrystaLLM's recipe trained on the whole corpus, on all four tasks. CrystaLLM's recipe, run on the same data and fine-tuned the same way, is worse than our single pass on 31 of the 32 task–budget pairs.

5Which Components Matter?

To see what drives the gain, we remove one component at a time and retrain the best configuration at each budget. For the comparison without ensembling, we use the best single snapshot of the full run (Figure 6).

5.1Snapshot Ensembling Helps Everywhere

Averaging snapshots helps in every domain. For molecules, the best single snapshot is worth 3.2–4.1× the data while the ensemble reaches 5.4–6.2×. At 5M–50M crystal tokens, those ranges are 5.0–9.9× and 7.4–13.3×. For DNA, averaging adds about 1–2× at most budgets; at 10M the difference is within seed noise.

5.2Looping Matters More as Data Grows

Reusing the core blocks helps more as the dataset grows. Removing looping costs 0.45× at 5M molecular tokens and 1.33× at 200M. For DNA, the cost grows from 0.9× at 20M to 3.4× at 200M. Looping also helps at every crystal budget we tested, by 0.8–2.3×.

5.3The Additions Depend on the Domain

Chain distillation adds 0.4–0.6× for molecules at 50M tokens and below. It adds little for DNA, and adding it to the crystal recipe did not help. Weight perturbation helps measurably only for crystals at 10M.

6Conclusion

Molecules, DNA and crystals look very different to a language model, yet the same core recipe of looped depth and an ensemble of training snapshots helps in all three. One run performs like a single pass with roughly five to thirteen times as much unique data, and against the published recipes for DNA and crystals it reaches the same loss with about twenty times less data. The gain carries over to downstream tasks, with LoRA fine-tuning and zero-shot. Fine-tuned, the recipe overtakes Xu et al.'s best released model from 100M unique tokens, Evo 2's recipe on the GUE benchmarks from 20M, and CrystaLLM's recipe trained on the whole corpus from 50M.

There are many interesting open questions. The first is how far the gain continues beyond 200M unique tokens, where our comparisons increasingly exceed the longest measured single pass. The second is why looping matters more as the dataset grows. Chen et al. find that looping regularises multi-epoch training and becomes compute-optimal as models grow, which suggests testing more loops at larger budgets. The third is how broadly the recipe transfers; future posts in this series will test further scientific domains.

7Related Work

Muennighoff et al. describe how the value of training data changes as it is repeated. For protein language models, Baron et al. show how Muon and strong weight decay improve multi-epoch training, and propose snapshot ensembling and chain distillation as next steps.

Snapshot ensembles (Huang et al.) collect several models from one run using a cyclic schedule (Loshchilov and Hutter). Training a model on an earlier model's predictions builds on Hinton et al. and Furlanello et al.. q0 brings these ideas together for repeated-data pretraining. Reusing blocks across depth dates to the Universal Transformer (Dehghani et al.), and Chen et al. introduce the loop-boundary operator used here.

Xu et al. study SMILES language models. Evo 2 (Brixi et al.) is trained on OpenGenome2, the source of our DNA data. CrystaLLM (Antunes et al.) generates crystal structures as CIF text. Our downstream evaluations draw on MoleculeNet (Wu et al.), Matbench (Dunn et al.) and ClinVar (Landrum et al.).

Appendix

AFull Pretraining Results

BWhat More Compute Can Buy

The main results use one run per data budget. With more compute, we can train several runs on the same data and combine their predictions. Snapshots within one run tend to be similar. Separate runs can make different errors because they use different seeds, weight decays, model shapes, pass counts, or recipe components. Combining them can take advantage of those differences.

Adaptive mixture. We include every second snapshot for DNA and every fourth for crystals from every run at a budget. Each included model is a member $j$ with prediction $p_j$. The mixture predicts $p(x_t) = \sum_j w_{t,j}\, p_j(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. We initialise the weights from a prior $\pi$ fitted with EM, then update them after each token in a document:

$$w_{t+1,j} = (1-\alpha)\,\frac{w_{t,j}\,p_j(x_t \mid x_{<t})^{\beta}}{\sum_i w_{t,i}\,p_i(x_t \mid x_{<t})^{\beta}} + \alpha\,\pi_j,$$
(B1)

A member gains weight when it predicts the current document well; $\alpha$ keeps some weight near the prior. We fit the prior and $(\beta, \alpha)$ on data that we do not use for scoring. For DNA, that is the validation genera. For crystals, we fit on one half of the held-out CIFs and score on the other, then reverse the halves. Setting $\beta = 0$ gives a weighted ensemble with fixed weights.

DNA. Mixing 8–11 runs raises the multiplier from 5.5–8.9× for one run to 10.0–14.0×. On the Evo 2 curve, the mixture is worth 25–31×. Adapting weights within each document supplies much of the gain: fixed weights reach only 8.7–10.4×. At 5M–20M, even three seeds of one recipe variant reach 8.4–10.4×. From 50M onward, mixtures of looped runs do better than mixtures without looping.

Crystals. The mixture adds 0.7–1.4× at 5M–25M and no measurable gain at 50M, despite combining 28–91 runs. Most runs have the same tuned model shape and make similar errors. On CrystaLLM's curve, the crystal mixture is worth 24.5–25.7× at 5M–25M. We have not run this cross-fitted mixture for molecules.

Compute cost. Training a mixture costs the sum of its runs, and prediction evaluates every member on every token. Extra runs help less once their errors become similar. When spending more compute, runs with different seeds, weight decays, shapes, or recipe components are more useful than repeats of the same configuration.

CTraining Setup

Every domain uses the same recipe and the same training setup. We train decoder-only transformers from scratch, packing whole documents into rows with a document mask so that the number of unique training tokens $U$ is exact. Muon updates the matrix parameters and AdamW the embeddings and the output head, without warmup or dropout. Each run makes many passes over its $U$ unique tokens. After an optional flat phase at the peak learning rate, training is divided into 64 cycles; within each cycle the learning rate falls linearly from its peak to $0.08\times$ while weight decay rises from $0.7\times$ to $1\times$, and the weights at the end of the cycle are saved as a snapshot. Predictions average the snapshots' next-token probabilities. The model is looped: a prelude block, a core of blocks applied two or three times with shared weights and the boundary operator of Chen et al. at each loop boundary, and a coda block.

Per domain and budget we tune only the model size, the number of passes, the peak learning rate and the weight decay; the cycle shape, the 64 snapshots and the loop count stay fixed. The product of learning rate and weight decay matters most: if it is too small, the snapshots memorise the training data, and its best value falls as the budget grows.

Snapshot ensembling and looped depth are used everywhere. Ensembling gives the largest gain for molecules and crystals and adds 1–2× for DNA at most budgets; the gain from looping grows with the budget for molecules, carries the DNA gain from 20M on and helps crystals at every budget (Section 5). The two further components are kept only where they help. Chain distillation adds 0.4–0.6× for molecules at budgets up to 50M and at most 0.4× for DNA, and never helped crystals, so it is not used there. Weight perturbation is used for molecules together with distillation and for crystals, where it helps at 10M; it gave nothing for DNA.
